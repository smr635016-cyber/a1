From Dock to Plate: Choosing and Prepping Fresh Seafood for the Grill

9 min read · Spice Grill Cove Kitchen Notes

Seafood is less forgiving on a grill than almost anything else you'll cook. There's a narrow window between properly cooked and overdone, and unlike a steak, you usually can't rescue a piece of fish that's gone too far. Most of the work that determines whether a grilled seafood dish turns out well actually happens before it ever touches the fire — at the counter, choosing what to buy, and at the cutting board, prepping it properly.

What freshness actually looks and smells like

The idea that fresh fish "shouldn't smell like fish" is mostly right, but it's worth being specific about what that means. Fresh fish should smell clean, mild, and slightly like the ocean — not sharp, sour, or heavily "fishy." Beyond smell, look at the eyes if you're buying whole fish: they should be clear and slightly bulging, not cloudy or sunken. The flesh should look firm and spring back when pressed gently, rather than leaving an indentation. For fillets, the flesh should look moist and slightly translucent rather than dull, dry, or separating along the muscle lines, which is often a sign it's been sitting too long.

For shellfish like mussels or clams, the shells should be closed, or close when tapped lightly — an open shell that doesn't close is a sign the shellfish is no longer alive and shouldn't be used. Shrimp should smell clean and the shell should look glossy rather than dull or slightly slimy.

Match the cut to the fire

Whole fish and thick, dense fillets — like snapper or similar firm, meaty fish — handle direct, hot grilling well because they have enough structure to hold together and enough time on the fire to develop real char before overcooking. Thin, delicate fillets, on the other hand, are much better suited to a hotter but shorter cook, or to being grilled on a plank or in a grill basket, since they can fall apart or overcook in the time it takes to get a whole fish properly charred.

Shellfish generally cooks fastest of all. Shrimp and scallops can go from perfectly cooked to rubbery in a minute or two, so it helps to have everything else ready — sides, plates, any finishing sauce — before they go on the grill, rather than trying to multitask while they cook.

Scoring, not just seasoning

For whole fish especially, a few diagonal cuts through the skin on both sides serves a real purpose beyond looking nice: it helps heat penetrate more evenly to the center, helps a rub or marinade reach past the skin, and reduces the chance of the skin splitting unevenly from trapped steam as it cooks. The cuts don't need to be deep — just enough to break through the skin and slightly into the flesh.

Drying the surface matters more than most people expect

Wet fish doesn't sear — it steams, and steaming is what causes fish to stick to the grill grate and shed its skin instead of releasing cleanly once it's actually done. Pat fish and shellfish dry with a paper towel before seasoning, and if time allows, let it sit uncovered in the refrigerator for twenty to thirty minutes before cooking so surface moisture can evaporate further. This single step does more for grill marks and clean release than almost any other adjustment.

Oil the fish, not just the grate

Oiling the grill grate helps, but a light coat of oil directly on the fish or shellfish itself makes a bigger difference for sticking, especially for delicate fillets or shellfish with less natural fat. Do this right before the food goes on the grill, after any dry rub has had time to sit, so the rub isn't washed away or turned into a paste.

Know when to stop touching it

The most common mistake with grilled seafood isn't undercooking — it's over-handling. Constant flipping, pressing, or checking breaks apart delicate flesh and prevents a proper sear from forming. Fish is usually ready to flip when it releases cleanly from the grate on its own; if it's sticking, it typically needs another minute. Most fillets need only one flip. Shellfish like shrimp or scallops often need only a single flip as well, given how quickly they cook through.

Reading doneness without a thermometer

While a thermometer is the most reliable tool, a lot of experienced cooks judge fish doneness visually: the flesh should turn from translucent to opaque and separate easily into flakes with light pressure from a fork, without falling apart entirely. Shellfish like shrimp curl into a loose "C" shape and turn opaque and slightly firm when done — a shrimp curled into a tight "O" shape is usually a sign it's gone slightly too far.

Buying frozen when fresh isn't a realistic option

Fresh isn't always available depending on where you live, and good-quality frozen seafood is a genuinely reasonable option rather than a compromise, as long as it's thawed properly. The biggest mistake with frozen fish and shellfish is thawing it quickly at room temperature or under warm water, which unevenly warms the outside while the inside stays frozen, and encourages moisture loss once it does thaw. A slow thaw in the refrigerator, given enough time — usually the better part of a day for a thicker piece — keeps the texture much closer to fresh. If you're short on time, sealed frozen seafood can also be thawed reasonably well under a steady stream of cold running water, checked and turned periodically, though this takes more attention than simply planning ahead.

Once thawed, treat it the same way you would fresh: pat it dry thoroughly, since thawed seafood tends to hold extra surface moisture, and give it a little longer to air-dry in the refrigerator if time allows before it goes anywhere near the grill.

A quick note on portioning for the grill

Grilled seafood is easiest to manage when portions are reasonably uniform in thickness, since a thin end will overcook long before a thick end is ready. For fillets with a noticeable taper, it can help to fold the thinner tail end under itself before cooking, which evens out the effective thickness across the piece and makes the timing far more predictable.

Building a relationship with whoever you're buying from

One of the more overlooked ways to improve what ends up on the grill is simply talking to the person selling it. Asking when something came in, or which item they'd personally choose that day, often gets you a more honest answer than reading a label alone, since a knowledgeable seller usually knows which case just arrived and which has been sitting a little longer. Over time, buying from the same source regularly tends to pay off — you get a better sense of what's genuinely good on a given day, and it becomes easier to plan a menu around what's actually fresh rather than what you'd already decided to cook before checking.

The takeaway

Good grilled seafood starts with a good buying decision, not just good technique at the grill. Choose fish and shellfish that's genuinely fresh, match the cut to the right kind of heat, dry the surface properly, and resist the urge to handle it more than necessary once it's on the fire. Do those four things consistently, and the actual grilling becomes far more forgiving than it initially seems.
