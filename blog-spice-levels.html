Balancing Heat and Flavor: Understanding Spice Levels on Our Menu

7 min read · Spice Grill Cove Kitchen Notes

Heat is one of the most misunderstood parts of spiced cooking. A lot of people either treat it as the whole point of a dish or avoid spiced food altogether because they assume "spiced" automatically means "very hot." In our kitchen, heat is treated as one ingredient among several — important, but never meant to overpower everything else on the plate. Here's how we actually think about it, and how to navigate our menu if you're not sure what your tolerance is.

Heat and flavor are not the same thing

It's easy to conflate "spicy" with "flavorful," but a dish can be intensely seasoned without being hot at all, and a dish can be very hot while barely being seasoned in any other way. Our ember chili blend, for example, is built primarily for smoky depth and color, with the heat as a secondary effect of using real chilies rather than the main goal. A dish built around raw heat alone tends to fatigue the palate quickly and mask the other work that's gone into it — the char, the salt, the aromatics — which is why we build heat in gradually rather than maximizing it.

How we think about our own heat scale

Rather than a single universal "spice level," we think about heat in relation to what a dish is trying to do. A finishing element like a pickled cove pepper is meant to add a bright, contained burst of heat you can control by how much you use, while a rub that's cooked into the food, like our ember chili blend, spreads more evenly and can't be adjusted once it's on the plate. When in doubt, ask your server which category a dish falls into — an "add if you want it" heat versus a "built in" heat — since that changes how much control you have over your own experience.

If you're sensitive to spice, here's what tends to work

  • Start with dishes built around turmeric, garlic, or citrus rather than chili — our cove spice half chicken, for example, leans on warmth and aromatics rather than heat.
  • Ask for sauces, pickles, or chili garnishes on the side rather than mixed in, which is almost always possible.
  • Vegetables and starches on the side act as a genuine buffer — plain grilled corn or bread can meaningfully soften the impact of a hotter dish eaten alongside it.
  • Dairy-based sides, when available, are more effective at cutting heat than water, which mostly just spreads capsaicin around rather than neutralizing it.

If you're looking for real heat

For guests who actively want more heat than what's built into a dish, we're generally able to add extra chili or a hotter pickled pepper on request, since the base seasoning is built to be adjustable rather than fixed. It's worth asking your server directly, since not every dish can safely be pushed hotter without throwing off the rest of the balance — a delicate fish dish, for instance, has much less room for added heat than a heartier grilled meat dish before the chili starts to dominate everything else.

Why we don't publish a numbered heat scale

Numbered heat scales look precise but tend to be inconsistent between kitchens and even between batches, since fresh chilies vary naturally in heat from one harvest to the next. Rather than assign a number that might not hold up meal to meal, we'd rather describe a dish honestly — "a gentle background warmth" versus "a dish built around real heat" — and let our staff help guide specific questions, since they taste the current batch of rubs and marinades directly.

Ordering for a table with mixed preferences

One of the more common situations we see is a table where one guest wants real heat and another would rather avoid it entirely, which can feel like it forces a compromise on everyone. In practice, it rarely does — because heat in our kitchen is mostly added at the finishing stage rather than baked uniformly into a shared dish, a table can often order the same core dishes and simply customize how much heat goes on each plate individually. Shared items like the fire-charred vegetable board or charred corn are naturally heat-neutral on their own, which makes them easy anchors for a mixed table, with any heat added afterward per plate rather than per dish.

A note for guests cooking heat-sensitive dishes at home

If you're recreating something similar to our approach at home for a group with different heat tolerances, the same logic applies: build your base seasoning around aromatics and salt rather than chili, and treat any chili as a garnish added at the table rather than mixed into the cooking process itself. This gives everyone control over their own plate without requiring you to cook multiple separate versions of the same dish, which is often the more practical solution than trying to guess a single heat level that will satisfy an entire table.

What actually determines how "hot" something tastes

Perceived heat isn't only about how much chili went into a dish — it's also shaped by what's served alongside it, how the dish is seasoned otherwise, and even the temperature of the food itself. A hot dish served with plenty of acidity or a cooling side tends to feel more balanced than the same level of chili served on its own with nothing to offset it, which is part of why our menu rarely serves a heavily spiced element in isolation. Similarly, a dish served very hot in temperature will often read as spicier than the same seasoning served warm, since heat and spiciness are processed by some of the same sensory pathways. None of this is an exact science in a home or restaurant kitchen, but it's part of why two dishes with a similar amount of chili can feel noticeably different to eat.

Kids and heat-sensitive guests at the same table

Families with children are one of the more common versions of the mixed-table situation, and the same approach applies: order the base dishes that lean on aromatics rather than chili, and add heat separately for the adults who want it. Our cove spice half chicken and grilled shrimp skewers both work well as shared dishes in this setup, since neither relies on chili as its defining flavor, which means a child and an adult who wants more heat can eat from the same plate with the heat added only where it's wanted.

How our kitchen actually calibrates a new dish

When a new dish is being developed, heat is one of the last things adjusted, not one of the first. We settle on the base seasoning, the cooking method, and the balance of salt and acidity before deciding how much chili, if any, the dish actually needs. This order matters because a dish with strong underlying flavor often needs less heat than expected to feel complete, while a dish with weak underlying seasoning tends to get pushed toward more chili to compensate for something that heat alone can't fix. Working in this order keeps heat as a deliberate choice rather than a default reach when a dish tastes unfinished.

The takeaway

Heat is a tool, not the goal, in how we cook. If you're cautious about spice, there's a real path through our menu that doesn't involve avoiding half the dishes — and if you want more heat, that's almost always something we can accommodate. Either way, the best approach is simply to ask; our staff would rather talk through a dish with you than have you guess and be surprised.
